Chapter 2 Using HumanIslets.com

The platform’s left-hand navigation groups features into four blocks — Omics, Donor & Phenotype, Search & Discovery, and Data Download. This chapter is organized in the same order, with one section per page in the sidebar.

2.1 Omics

The Omics block holds the analyses that operate primarily on feature-level molecular data: differential-expression-style omics analysis and multi-omics integration.

2.1.1 Omics View

HumanIslets.com presently includes information from 625 human research islet isolations that includes donor and technical metadata, islet and islet cell function, and omics. Omics datasets include bulk proteomics, bulk RNAseq, gene panels (Nanostring), patch-seq, pseudobulk RNAseq for alpha- and beta-cells, and DNA methylation (Illumina EPICv2 array). Omics-metadata relationships can be analysed by:

  1. Selecting the ‘Omics Type’ of interest
  2. Selecting the ‘Primary Metadata’ to be analysed, and ‘Comparison of Interest’ if applicable
  3. Choosing covariates to control for
    1. Many covariates are available for correction and selection may depend on comparisons being made or the objective of the user. Note however we have reported important impacts of correcting for ‘culture time’, particularly when examining RNAseq data, and ‘Non-endocrine cell proportion (computed)’ when comparing groups with dramatically different preparation purities.
  4. Selecting donors to include
    1. Choosing ‘Subset’ will open a window that allows for filtering by donor characteristics and data availability, or upload of a list of donor IDs or RRIDs of interest
  5. Selecting a p-value cutoff, and the use of adjusted p-values
  6. Click ‘submit’

The number of donors included in the analysis will be displayed. If too few donors are available for the chosen analysis (<10), an error message will be shown. Results of the analysis will be shown under ‘Feature Association Results’, and results can be downloaded with Download Results. Results are shown as a graphical representation (volcano plot) or table, accessible by clicking on the appropriate tab. Clicking on features (points in the volcano plot, or table entries) will raise a graph showing categorical or scatterplot representations of the data/feature of interest. Clicking on individual donors within those plots will take you to the ‘Donor View’ page for that donor, allowing for inspection of potential outlier donors. At the right are links to the NCBI gene page and T2D Knowledge Portal (T2DKP) entries for each feature (external sites), to a single-cell RNAseq reference dataset (SC) to inspect cell-type specific expression of the feature, and to IsletAgent (KG link; section 2.3.1) for the evidence about the feature.

Wilcoxon check. For a two-group comparison with no covariates, the results table also shows a Wilcoxon rank-sum test next to limma, as a check that does not assume normally distributed data. Three columns are added at the end: Wilcoxon AUC (the chance that a donor in one group has a higher value than a donor in the other; above 0.5 means higher in the group where log2FC is positive, and 0.5 means no difference), Wilcoxon p_value and Wilcoxon adjusted p_value. The check runs only when both groups have enough donors, and not for DNA methylation. Because the Wilcoxon test cannot adjust for covariates, it is not shown when covariates are selected, including the batch covariate that is added automatically for some datasets.

DNA methylation is analysed with the same association framework, but at the level of individual CpG sites rather than genes: differential analysis is run on M-values (log2 methylation ratios), while the per-feature boxplots show β-values (the 0–1 methylation proportion). Each result row shows the CpG’s genomic location, its annotated gene, and its CpG-island relation. Methylation is profiled at roughly 729,000 CpG sites, so the analysis takes a little longer than other omics, and a methylation-only Regions (DMR) tab additionally groups neighbouring CpGs into differentially methylated regions.

The third tab under the ‘Feature Association Results’ pulls up a Pathway Analysis tool. The enrichment method offered depends on the omics type, because gene-based, metabolite-based, and reaction-based feature lists each require matched annotation libraries:

  • Gene-based omics (RNA-seq, Nanostring, proteomics, pseudobulk, patch-seq): Overrepresentation Analysis (ORA) and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) against KEGG, Reactome, GO, and the GEM gene libraries.
  • DNA methylation: gene-set ORA and GSEA, run through a methylation-aware engine: each CpG is mapped to its annotated gene(s) and the enrichment is corrected for the number of CpG probes per gene (genes covered by more probes would otherwise be biased toward looking enriched). ORA uses missMethyl’s gsameth and GSEA uses methylGSA, against the same gene-based libraries (KEGG, Reactome, GO, and the GEM gene sets); reaction-level GEM sets do not apply.
  • Metabolomics: MSEA (Metabolite Set Enrichment Analysis), the metabolomics analogue of GSEA, implemented via xialab’s MetaboAnalyst and ranking metabolites against curated metabolite sets (KEGG human metabolite-centred pathways and the GEM metabolite groupings).
  • Metabolic flux: RSEA (Reaction Set Enrichment Analysis), ranking reactions against Human-GEM subsystem groupings (glycolysis, TCA cycle, fatty-acid metabolism, etc.). This is the analogue of GSEA at the reaction level.

To perform pathway / metabolite / reaction analysis:

  1. Select ORA or GSEA / MSEA / RSEA as appropriate for the omics type
    1. ORA is performed on significant hits based on the p-value threshold set above
    2. GSEA / MSEA / RSEA can be performed using log2FC/coefficient or T-statistic for ranking, and can be performed even in cases when no individual features are marked as significant
  2. Select whether to collapse redundant pathways / sets
  3. Choose a false discovery rate (FDR) cutoff
  4. Choose among the available libraries for the matched omics type
  5. Click ‘submit’

The ‘Pathway Results’ will be displayed in both graphical and table format, selected by clicking on the appropriate tab. As above, results can be downloaded with Download Results. In the interactive graphical view, significant pathways (based on FDR cutoff) are shown as ridgeplots. Clicking on these will raise heatmaps showing the contribution of individual genes/proteins.

2.1.2 Multi-omics View

The Multi-omics View lets users explore integrated analyses across two or three omics layers selected from a single dropdown (WGCNA and MOFA results are precomputed; a combination is offered only when enough donors have every selected omics type). Three complementary methods are available, each answering a slightly different question and unlocked by selecting it from the Method dropdown:

  • WGCNA (Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis) is unsupervised. It groups features that move together across donors into modules, then summarises each module by a single representative value (its eigengene) so that module-level signal can be compared across omics and against the platform’s phenotype variables. Use WGCNA when the question is which features behave like a co-regulated programme, and does any module track a phenotype?
  • MOFA (Multi-Omics Factor Analysis) is also unsupervised, but rather than discrete modules it learns continuous latent factors — directions of variation that several omics share. The same factor often explains a substantial chunk of variance in two or more views, which makes MOFA a good fit when the goal is to find the shared axes of variation across omics types. Donors with only partial omics coverage are still usable thanks to MOFA’s NA-tolerant fit.
  • DIABLO (Data Integration Analysis for Biomarker discovery using Latent cOmponents) is supervised: it requires a primary metadata variable as the target and finds the smallest set of correlated features across omics that best discriminates two groups (or predicts a continuous outcome). Use DIABLO when the question is phrased as which combined feature signature best separates these donor groups?

A help (?) tooltip next to the dropdown summarises these three methods inline. Full methodology and parameter choices are in chapter 5; here we walk through how to use each method on the page.

To run a multi-omics analysis:

  1. Pick the Method (WGCNA, MOFA, or DIABLO) from the dropdown
  2. Choose Omics Type I and Omics Type II (and optionally Omics Type III) — the combinations are precomputed, so the choices that have ≥ 20 shared donors will be selectable
    1. For patch-seq, an extra cell type selector appears (α / β / δ / PP)
    2. For metabolite and flux, an extra glucose condition selector appears (low / high / ratio)
  3. For DIABLO only, pick a Primary Metadata variable and (for binary outcomes) the two contrast levels, and optionally covariates under Adjust for. WGCNA and MOFA are unsupervised and skip this step.
  4. Choose Donors: All, or Subset to use the donor filter
  5. Click Submit

The result tabs (described below per method) then become available. WGCNA and MOFA results load almost instantly because they are precomputed; DIABLO runs when you click Submit and can take longer.

WGCNA results layout. A summary bar shows the number of donors, features and modules, and the scale-free fit and power. Five tabs:

  1. Overview — a heatmap with modules on the rows and core phenotypes on the columns. Each cell shows the module–phenotype association: Pearson r (signed, −1 to +1) for continuous and binary phenotypes, or ANOVA-derived effect size (unsigned, 0 to 1) for multi-level categoricals. Cells carry significance asterisks (* padj<0.05, ** <0.01, *** <0.001), and hovering any cell reveals the full r / p / padj / n. A Select Phenotypes button opens a dialog to choose which phenotypes appear on the columns (with a Reset to defaults shortcut), and the heatmap can be exported as PNG (image) or CSV (underlying matrix). This is the entry-point view for “which module tracks which phenotype, at a glance”.
  2. Trait Associations — drill-down view for a single phenotype chosen from the Group / Phenotype selectors; shows that phenotype’s association across every module in the combination (not just the curated “core” phenotype set in the Overview).
  3. Features — per-module feature table with module membership ranking and omics-type tags.
  4. Network — interactive node/edge view of the top topological-overlap edges, with nodes coloured by omics type and modules.
  5. Pathway — ORA or GSEA (ranked by kME) for a selected module.

MOFA results layout. Seven tabs:

  1. Overview — a heatmap with MOFA factors on the rows and core phenotypes on the columns, using the same conventions as the WGCNA Overview: Pearson r for continuous/binary, effect size for multi-level categoricals, the same asterisk-based significance markers, the same hover detail, the same Select Phenotypes / Reset / PNG / CSV controls. This is the place to start when asking “which MOFA factor tracks which phenotype”.
  2. Variance Decomposition — per-factor per-view variance heatmap.
  3. Trait Associations — drill-down view for a single phenotype across every MOFA factor (analogue of WGCNA’s Trait Associations tab).
  4. Feature Loadings — top-weighted features per factor per omics.
  5. Circos Plot — the top-loading features of a factor in each omics, linked by their correlation.
  6. Factor Space — donors plotted in factor space, with metadata-colouring options.
  7. Pathway — pathway enrichment for a selected factor.

DIABLO results layout. Four tabs:

  1. Overview — Sample View (donors on the multi-omics components) and Total Variance (variance explained per component and omics).
  2. Important Features — loading plots per omics and the table of selected features, by component.
  3. Circos Plot — the selected features across omics, linked by their correlation.
  4. Pathway Analysis — ORA / GSEA on the selected features against the standard pathway libraries.

All tables are downloadable as CSV from the corresponding tab.

2.2 Donor & Phenotype

The Donor & Phenotype block groups the views that browse the cohort at the donor level: per-donor profiles, phenotype-only exploration, and multi-omics-derived donor stratification.

2.2.1 Donor View

The Donor View page allows examination of individual donor, islet isolation, and functional data from each of 625 donors currently in the HumanIslets.com database. Donors can be searched by ADI IsletCore Donor ID or Research Resource ID (RRID). Data categories can be selected from the left-hand menu, organized into four groups: Metadata (Donor Information, Isolation Information); Genetic Variables (Ancestry, Genetic Risk Score); Insulin Secretion (Static GSIS plus separate Dynamic Insulin Response views for Glucose, Leucine, and Palmitate); and Other (Prohormone Expression, Mitochondrial Function, Single-cell electrophysiology, Pancreas Lipid Extraction, and Omics Availability). These metadata are described in detail in chapters 3 and 4, below.

Typically, data is shown for selected donor in the context of the entire dataset, allowing for easy inspection and determination of whether a donor falls within the normal range of the population. A few points that are worth noting here:

  • Donor Information shows the RRID if available. Clicking on this will link out to the NCBI Biosample page for this donor
  • Isolation Information also contains the current inventory of snap frozen and cryopreserved islet tubes for that donor (all donors also have paraffin embedded biopsies)
  • Isolation Information includes cell type composition calculated by deconvolution in donors with bulk proteomics data (see section 5.6, below)
  • Ancestry renders the donor’s ancestry as a donut chart with primary ancestry on the outer ring and secondary (sub-) ancestry on the inner ring (1000 Genomes-aligned), accompanied by tables of the underlying probabilities
  • Genetic Risk Score shows the T1D Genetic Risk Score and the T2D Polygenic Risk Score, each with the donor’s overall score and position in the cohort distribution, plus partitioned risk (T1D: HLA DR-DQ, HLA class I, HLA class II and non-HLA; T2D: Hard or Soft clustering)
  • Dynamic Insulin Responses can be shown with or without normalisation to baseline
  • Mitochondrial Function (oxygen consumption by Seahorse assay) can be shown with or without normalisation to DNA and baseline
  • Prohormone Expression shows the donor’s C-peptide, proinsulin, and proinsulin:C-peptide ratio concentrations as values plus per-readout cohort histograms, with a selector to switch between the measured conditions
  • Pancreas Lipid Extraction shows the donor’s total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), and free cholesterol (FC) (mg/g tissue) as values plus per-lipid cohort histograms

2.2.2 Phenotype View

The Phenotype View lets users probe relationships among the 198 phenotype variables in the database (donor characteristics, isolation outcomes, secretion, mitochondrial function, perifusion, electrophysiology) without selecting any omics type. The page has a left-hand menu with three options:

  • Overview: a scrollable heatmap of every donor against every phenotype variable, with cells coloured by each donor’s z-score against the population, so at a glance users can see where a donor sits across the full phenotype panel and spot clusters of donors that look similar across many measurements at once. A Visual Filter radio group lets users restrict the view to a specific Donor subset (chosen via the standard donor filter dialog, by characteristics, data availability, or a donor-ID upload) or to a Phenotype subset (chosen via the phenotype filter dialog); clicking Update re-renders the heatmap with the chosen filter, and once a donor subset is applied a Download Donor List button appears so the same selection can be re-used on the Data Download page. Use this view to spot outliers, compare a few donors against the whole cohort, or quickly check which phenotype variables are populated for a candidate analysis cohort.

  • Correlation: an interactive chord diagram (and matching sortable table) of pairwise correlations among phenotype variables, kept only when they pass a user-set correlation-magnitude cutoff and a p-value cutoff. Users can restrict the analysis to a subset of donors or phenotypes and choose whether to include within-group correlations (off by default so the chord emphasises cross-group co-variation), then click Submit. The results panel has two tabs:

    • Chord Diagram — clicking any ribbon opens the scatter plot of the underlying donor-level values for that pair, so users can immediately see whether the correlation is driven by a linear trend, by an outlier cluster, or by something more complex; the dialog supports CSV / SVG / PNG / PDF export.
    • Correlation Table — every kept pair listed in a sortable, searchable table (green / red colour-coded for positive vs. negative r); clicking a row opens the same scatter dialog, and a Download CSV button exports the full list.

    Use this view to find phenotype variables that strongly co-vary, for example which insulin-secretion measures track each other or whether HbA1c correlates with electrophysiology readouts.

  • Phenotype PCA: Probabilistic PCA (PPCA) on the donor × phenotype matrix, which (unlike standard PCA) tolerates the missing-value pattern typical of phenotype data. Users pick which two principal components to plot, a Color by variable to colour the donor points (two-tier selector with variable group → variable name, mirroring the Omics page; continuous variables get a gradient, categorical variables a discrete palette), and optionally one categorical Shape by variable so two metadata dimensions can be encoded on the same scatter at once. Two additional controls fine-tune the fit — Label outliers (how many donors to label automatically by distance from the centroid) and Min samples (minimum non-missing measurements per phenotype to be included). After clicking Submit, the results panel has two tabs:

    • PCA Plot — donors in PC space, coloured / shaped per the chosen variables. Clicking any donor point opens a Donor Profile dialog showing the top 10 phenotype features where that donor deviates most from the population mean (blue = above, orange = below).
    • Feature Loadings — every phenotype’s loading on the chosen PCs, ranked by importance (sum of |loadings| on the two PCs), with a Show top N selector and CSV export.

    Use this view to identify the major axes of phenotypic variation in the cohort, to spot outlier donors, and to see which phenotype variables drive that separation.

2.2.3 Cluster View

The Cluster View groups donors into molecular clusters. The clusters come from bulk RNA-seq and proteomics, not from clinical labels. Donors without these omics data are assigned to a cluster from their phenotypes, and each assignment has a reliability level (high, medium or low). The full method is described in chapter 5 (Variational Autoencoder (VAE) for donor clustering). The page is called Cluster View in the sidebar, with the subtitle Donor cluster explorer.

Donor map. The map shows every donor. Each cluster has its own arm, and a donor sits nearer the clusters it belongs to more strongly: omics donors from their omics data, and donors without omics from their predicted probabilities. Clicking a donor point opens that donor’s de-identified record.

  • Color by / Shape by: colour the points by any phenotype or by cluster, and shape them by a categorical phenotype or by cluster.
  • Measured (omics) / Predicted: click either legend item to show or hide those donors.
  • Point size: sets the size of the points. Use the buttons to zoom in, zoom out or reset; scroll to zoom and drag to pan.
  • Phenotype directions: a button shows which measured phenotypes vary across the map, as labels around it.
  • Border donors: highlights measured donors that sit between two clusters.

Cluster cards. A card on the right summarises each cluster: its name, a short description, the main phenotype and omics differences, and how many donors are measured or predicted. Selecting a card opens the cluster detail page (below).

Comparing two groups. The Omics DE and Phenotype buttons open a panel that compares two groups of donors. Click a cluster to add it to Group A or Group B; Group B can also be all other clusters. For Omics DE, choose the omics type: RNA-seq, Proteomics, Nanostring, Pseudobulk (alpha), Pseudobulk (beta), Metabolomics (LG), Metabolomics (HG), Metabolomics (HG/LG) or Methylation. Advanced options let you adjust for covariates (regressed out with a linear model in limma), choose which donors to include (Omics only, + High conf., + High & Med or + All predicted), and limit donors with filters. Click Run to start, or Clear to start over.

Cluster phenotype heatmap. Rows are phenotypes, grouped by category, and columns are clusters. Each cell shows Cohen’s d for the cluster against all other clusters (red = higher, blue = lower), with asterisks marking significance. Only phenotypes that reach nominal significance in at least one cluster are shown. The Donors filter sets which donors are used: Omics only, + High conf., + High & Med or + All predicted. Click a cell for details.

Cluster detail page (#/cluster-detail/…, opened from a cluster card). Each cluster has its own page with these sections:

  • Header: the cluster name, a short summary, a plain-language description, and how many donors have omics data or are predicted. Back to Cluster View returns to the main page.
  • Demographics: the cluster’s mean and range for Age, BMI and HbA1c (%) against the whole cohort, and the number of male and female donors.
  • Diabetes Status: donor counts by Diabetes diagnosis (raw) and by Corrected diabetes status, side by side. Corrected diabetes status is derived from HbA1c, not a clinical re-diagnosis.
  • Data Availability: how many donors in the cluster have each omics type and each phenotype group.
  • Phenotype Enrichment: phenotypes compared between the cluster and all other clusters, with effect size, p-value, adjusted p-value, number of donors, and the mean in and outside the cluster. Advanced comparison changes the comparison, and Reset table goes back to the default.
  • Omics Differential Expression: choose an omics type to see features that differ between the cluster and all other clusters (limma, unadjusted by default). The table shows log2FC, t, p-value and adjusted p-value. The default view shows the top features; Run full DE runs the full analysis, Advanced comparison changes the groups, covariates and donors, and Reset to default goes back.
  • Pathway Enrichment: pre-ranked GSEA on the t statistics of the current comparison, against GO Molecular Function, KEGG and Reactome. The table shows NES, p-value, adjusted p-value, hits and genes. Run on current comparison updates it after you change the comparison.
  • KG links: in both omics tables, the KG link opens that feature in IsletAgent.
  • Explore Further: cards linking to Cluster View, Omics Analysis, Multi-omics and IsletAgent.

2.3 Search & Discovery

The Search & Discovery block groups feature-centric and entity-centric exploration: ask a question about a gene/phenotype, or jump from one feature to all its associations.

2.3.1 IsletAgent

IsletAgent answers plain-language questions about a gene, a phenotype, or the relationship between them. Each answer is grounded in the HumanIslets cohort, in public and local genetics, and in public knowledge, all held in the platform’s knowledge graph (chapter 6). The page is labelled BETA.

Asking a question. You can ask about genes, proteins, metabolites and islet phenotypes, and the links between them. For example: whether a feature is related to a phenotype or disease, how features or pathways differ between donor groups such as diabetes status or molecular clusters, or how well an omics layer predicts a phenotype. Type a question in the box and click Ask, or click one of the example questions under Try, such as Is GCK linked to type 2 diabetes?, What predicts HbA1c (%)? or Which pathways are enriched in type 2 diabetes in RNA-seq? Click Watch an example to see a full answer step by step. While IsletAgent works, a progress bar shows each step: Understood, Chose, Found, Ran and Result.

How it works. IsletAgent is a small language model, fine-tuned on grounded examples from the knowledge graph and run privately on local hardware. It reads the question, picks the entities and a retrieval route, and fetches the evidence from the knowledge graph through reviewed query templates. When a question needs a new calculation, it calls one of the platform’s analyses, such as differential expression, phenotype association, pathway enrichment, colocalization or Mendelian randomization. Every retrieval and calculation can be checked and reproduced.

Reading an answer. Each answer names its source: the data, the analysis and the number of donors behind it.

  • Evidence verdict: the card at the top names the relationship (for example, GCK → type 2 diabetes) and gives a one-word verdict (for example, Convergent). It lists which sources agree (cohort, genetics, public knowledge) and shows the evidence grade (Limited, Moderate, Strong or Very Strong) with its evidence score. The grade scores the relationship, not the gene alone. A low grade means there is little evidence so far, not that the link is wrong.
  • Overview tab: the evidence, shown as panels. Each panel can be moved, widened, collapsed or closed, and its ? button explains it. Which panels appear depends on the question:
    • Evidence profile: how much each kind of evidence contributes, against its maximum: Association, Prediction, cis-QTL, GWAS, Mendelian randomization and Knowledge.
    • Donor-level measurement: each donor’s level of the feature, one panel per platform, split by the phenotype (for example, Diabetes diagnosis or Corrected diabetes status), with covariate-adjusted significance.
    • Prediction: the multivariate models (random forest, XGBoost, elastic net) that rank the feature as a predictor, showing where it ranks and how well each model predicts.
    • What the cohort holds: how many donors have the feature on each platform, and its distribution.
    • Traits linked to …: the traits most strongly associated with the gene in public genetics, with their HuGE evidence tier.
    • Knowledge-graph context: only the curated public relations that link the two entities (PrimeKG, Hetionet and pathway libraries).
    • Literature: published mentions, with PMID and title. These are pointers only; they are never scored and never sent to the model.
  • Methods & data tab:
    • Results: every result behind the answer as a table, with View and CSV.
    • Methods and input data: the statistical method behind each calculation, the data it used, and an R script + data download that reproduces it locally.
      • Cohort · differential is a limma linear model per feature, phenotype and platform, adjusted for age, sex, BMI and culture time (none for the metabolite and contaminant layers), with Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p-values.
      • Cohort · prediction uses random forest, XGBoost and elastic net, with cross-validated or held-out model quality.
    • Public endpoint: the tab also shows the endpoint that returns the same answer (POST https://dev.humanislets.com/kg-api/agent/ask).
  • Guide: the Guide tab and Take a quick tour of this answer walk through each part of the answer.

Limits. IsletAgent answers only from the data and analyses HumanIslets supports. When the evidence is unclear, it gives a cautious answer. Use it to find and weigh evidence, not as proof of a mechanism.

2.3.2 Feature View

Instead of running an analysis in the ‘Omics View’ focused on a particular omics type and metadata variable, users can search for a feature of interest and pull up ALL associations for that particular feature of interest, corrected for common covariates (see section 5.5, below). Searchable features include genes/proteins and also metadata variable. This allows rapid interrogation of precomputed results across all datasets.

Searching for a gene/protein feature generates a table of results ranked by adjusted p-value. Searching for a metadata variable (e.g. BMI) generates a table of feature (gene/protein) results across all omics datasets, ranked by adjusted p-value. Clicking on rows and external links brings up categorical or scatterplots of the association, external pages for the feature of interest, or single-cell RNAseq expression for that feature. Because a large number of associations can be displayed, filtering of the metadata or omics type is enabled by clicking on the column title.

2.4 Data Download

The Data Download page is a standalone top-level entry in the sidebar; it does not belong to one of the analytical categories above.

2.4.1 Data Download

Most data at HumanIslets.com can be downloaded. Bulk RNA-seq, proteomics and genetic data are available on request until publication, and patch-seq data are reached through a separate link on the page. Donors of interest can be filtered by Donor Characteristics (see section 3.1) and/or data availability. Advanced filters allow selection of donors of interest by any metadata available. Donors of interest can also be selected by uploading a list of donor IDs (for example, donor IDs obtained from a particular Omics View analysis). Data can be downloaded as Comma separated values (.csv), text (.txt), R object (.rds), or in Excel format (.xlsx) within a zip file. To perform data download:

  1. Under ‘Select Donors’ choose filtering parameters, upload a list of donor IDs
    1. Distribution of chosen donors compared with the entire dataset can be assessed by clicking on the icons at right
  2. Once donors of interest are selected/filtered, click ‘submit’ to choose those donors
    1. The total number of donors chosen will be displayed
  3. Under ‘Download Data’
    1. Select the datasets of interest
    2. Select the data format
    3. The distribution of datasets selected, compared with the entire database, can be shown by clicking ‘Summary’
  4. Click ‘Download’
